01

The finding

A complaint reaches us named after the symptom the customer can see. Reading the 15 cases together, that name turns out to be a poor guide to the fault.

14 of 15 cases arrived labelled as a TV or camera fault. Only 3 of them actually were. The rest were ordinary access-network faults — a dead Wi-Fi unit, a mis-configured SSID, a bad cable crimp, weak coverage, an ISP outage, an out-of-range optical level. The television was the detector, not the defect. It is the one device in the house that is on every evening, so it is where an intermittent connection is felt first and reported.

The consequence is operational, not semantic. A case labelled “TV issue” gets routed and triaged as a customer-premise problem, which is the slowest possible path for what is usually our own device failing.

The Wi-Fi device is the single largest cause. 4 of 15 cases ended in a unit being replaced, and in every one of those the symptom was the same: the network name had vanished from every device in the house. A reset was tried in those cases and never recovered it — the unit was dead, not confused.
03

Were the replacements necessary?

Every one of the 4 swaps was raised on the same symptom: the network name had disappeared. That symptom has two very different causes — a dead radio, or an SSID the television cannot see while every other device can. The two are separable after the fact, because a dead unit stops pinging and a configuration fault does not. Each swap was tested against the hourly device ping record.

On SY048096 the router was carrying clients on both 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz on the day it was swapped, and was broadcasting all three of its SSIDs. Replacing it worked, but only because a new unit comes up with a fresh WLAN configuration. We changed the hardware to fix a setting.

Band steering is measured on every device and the flag was never set on any of these four, so this is not the platform steering clients between bands. It is the narrower problem of a television failing to detect an SSID that is plainly being broadcast — the same fault family as the two cases that were fixed by renaming the network, with no visit and no hardware.

What this costs. On the evidence available, at least one of the 4 swaps was avoidable, and the honest reading of the remaining three is one confirmed and two unknown. A router costs materially more than a remote SSID change, and the swap needs a CSP visit, so the process gap is not cosmetic. The check that separates them takes seconds and is already in our own data.

05

Why it keeps happening

Three patterns run underneath the 15 cases. Each is a process gap, not a one-off.

  1. We diagnose the appliance, not the network. The complaint arrives named after the TV, so the first hour goes into the TV. In 12 of 15 cases that hour was spent on the wrong side of the wall.
  2. We cannot tell a dead unit from a hidden SSID in the field. Both present as “the network name isn't showing”. 4 units were replaced on that symptom and, where it can be checked, at least one was online and broadcasting at the time. The ping record answers this in seconds and nobody consults it before authorising a swap.
  3. We close on the customer's word, not on a measurement. Coverage, optical power and ISP throughput each caused a case here, and none of the three is visible to the CSP standing in the room. Where it was measured — −30 dBm on one line, a speed shortfall on another — the cause was found immediately.
06

What we are changing

A remote-first triage drawn from these 15 cases. It is written so a support agent can run it on the call, before any visit is booked.

  1. Ask what the customer can see, not what is broken. “Is the Wi-Fi name showing in the TV's network list?” splits these 15 cases almost in half. Name missing means a device or configuration fault; name present means speed, coverage or the TV itself.
  2. Name missing → check the ping record before raising a swap. If the router is pinging and shows clients on either band, the unit is alive and the fault is the SSID, not the hardware. Only a unit that has genuinely stopped reporting should be replaced.
  3. Name visible on the phone but not the TV → rename the SSID from WLAN settings. This closed a case remotely with no visit, and is the open action on one more.
  4. TV joins nothing while the phone is fine → check the band before booking anyone. Older sets are 2.4 GHz only. One customer needed a TV service centre, not us.
  5. Buffering rather than disconnection → read the line first. Optical receive power and ISP throughput, before dispatch. −30 dBm is already out of range and no CSP visit will move it.
  6. Camera down while Wi-Fi is up → test the cable run to the DVR. Where the DVR sits in a different shop, the RJ45 crimp is the first suspect.
  7. Close no-fault cases explicitly as customer-premise. 3 of 15 were the customer's own equipment. Unless they are dispositioned as such they keep ageing in the tracker and are counted as our breach.
The measurable test of this change is the split above: today 80% of “TV issues” are network faults being triaged as appliance faults. If the triage works, that mislabelling should fall, and the cases that remain should reach a device swap in hours rather than days.
